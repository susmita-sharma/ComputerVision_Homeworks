HW5 Step 1 · Optical Flow & Tracking on Video 2 videos · 30 s each

Upload two videos you captured, each with at least 30 seconds of motion (people walking, cars, a pet, a ball, or the camera itself panning). For each clip the app:

  1. computes dense optical flow (Farneback) between every pair of consecutive frames,
  2. follows ~150-200 corner features over time (KLT tracks),
  3. estimates the camera's own motion and removes it, so what remains is motion of objects,
  4. boxes each moving object, gives it an ID and follows it frame to frame,
  5. renders all of this into a video that plays back in real time, and writes the numbers behind the conclusions (CSV, plots, summary) into a downloadable ZIP.

Processing runs in the background at roughly 3-5x slower than real time (about 1.5-2.5 minutes for 30 s). You watch the annotated frames appear live while it runs. Phone videos at 60 fps are analysed at 30 fps.

How to read the output video (2×2 panels)

1 · Objects + KLT tracks (top-left). The original frame. Boxes = moving objects with ID, speed (px/frame) and an arrow for direction; thin lines = each object's centroid path. Dots and trails = tracked corner features: orange on a moving object, cyan on the background.
2 · Dense flow (HSV) (top-right). Each pixel's motion vector as a color: hue = direction (see the color wheel), brightness = speed. Black = no motion.
3 · Flow vectors (bottom-left). The same field as arrows on a 12 px grid, drawn 3× longer than the true motion.
4 · Camera-compensated motion (bottom-right). |flow − flow caused by the camera's own motion|. Green outlines are the regions above the adaptive threshold: these are what the app counts as objects.

What counts as an "object"?

No object detector or neural network is used. An object here is a connected region whose motion differs from the camera's motion by more than an adaptive threshold (median + 4 robust standard deviations of the residual, never below 1 px/frame), after removing small specks and keeping regions of at least 0.3% of the frame. The camera's motion is a similarity transform (shift, rotation, zoom) fitted with RANSAC to the KLT tracks, most of which sit on the static background. So a walking person, a car, or a waved hand becomes an object. A camera pan does not, because the whole background moves consistently and gets subtracted. One object can split in two when parts of it stop moving (e.g. a person's torso vs. legs), and two objects that touch can merge into one.

What optical flow measures, and what can be inferred from it

Optical flow \(\mathbf{v}(x,y) = (u,v)\) is the apparent 2D velocity of image brightness at each pixel between two frames, under brightness constancy \(I(x,y,t) = I(x+u,\,y+v,\,t+1)\). It is the projection of 3D motion onto the image, so from it we can infer:

  • Where and when things move: segmenting non-zero flow gives the moving regions and the times they move.
  • How fast and in which direction: \(|\mathbf v|\) in px/frame (× fps = px/s) and \(\operatorname{atan2}(v,u)\).
  • Camera ego-motion vs. object motion: a pan gives near-uniform flow over the whole frame, a zoom or forward walk gives a radially expanding field, while an object gives localized flow that disagrees with its surroundings.
  • Approach / recession: positive divergence \(\partial u/\partial x + \partial v/\partial y\) means expansion (the object is getting closer, which gives a time-to-contact cue), and negative means contraction.
  • Relative depth (when the camera translates): nearer surfaces move faster in the image (motion parallax).
  • Its own limits: on textureless surfaces the gradient is \(\approx 0\), so brightness constancy gives no constraint (aperture problem) and the flow there is just interpolated.

Each video's results below list these inferences with the measured numbers behind them. The derivation of the flow constraint is on the Tracking page.
