HW5 Step 2 · Motion Tracking, Bilinear Interpolation & Validation

Derives the two-frame point-tracking problem from first principles, then runs a from-scratch implementation and checks its predicted pixel locations against OpenCV's reference tracker on a real frame pair.

1. Brightness constancy → the optical flow equation

Assume a scene point keeps its brightness as it moves by \((dx, dy)\) over time \(dt\):

$$I(x+dx,\ y+dy,\ t+dt) = I(x,y,t)$$

First-order Taylor-expand the left side around \((x,y,t)\):

$$I(x,y,t) + I_x\,dx + I_y\,dy + I_t\,dt \approx I(x,y,t)$$

Divide by \(dt\) and let \(u=dx/dt,\ v=dy/dt\) be the flow velocity. The \(I(x,y,t)\) terms cancel, leaving the optical flow equation:

$$I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$$

This is one equation in two unknowns \((u,v)\) at a single pixel - the aperture problem: the gradient only constrains flow along the gradient direction, not perpendicular to it.

2. Lucas-Kanade: a window resolves the aperture problem

Assume \((u,v)\) is constant over a small window \(W\) (typically 7-21 px) around the point. Every pixel in \(W\) then gives one copy of the flow equation, turning it into an overdetermined least-squares problem:

$$\min_{u,v} \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \big(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\big)^2$$

Setting the gradient w.r.t. \((u,v)\) to zero gives the normal equations:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\sum I_x^2 & \sum I_xI_y \\ \sum I_xI_y & \sum I_y^2\end{bmatrix}}_{A^TA} \begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{-\begin{bmatrix}\sum I_xI_t \\ \sum I_yI_t\end{bmatrix}}_{-A^Tb} $$

\(A^TA\) is invertible exactly when the window has gradient energy in two independent directions - a corner-like patch, not a flat region or a single straight edge (the same 2x2 "structure tensor" Harris corner detection is built on).

3. Setting up the two-frame tracking problem

Given frame 1 \(I_1\), frame 2 \(I_2\), and a point \((x_0,y_0)\) in \(I_1\), tracking asks: find the displacement \((u,v)\) minimizing the sum-of-squared-differences over a window:

$$E(u,v) = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} \big(I_2(x+u,\ y+v) - I_1(x,y)\big)^2$$

\(I_t\) above is really the finite difference \(I_2 - I_1\), which is only a good linear approximation for small \((u,v)\). For a real displacement of several pixels, Newton's method is used: solve the LK normal equations for a correction, update \((u,v)\), re-sample \(I_2\) at the new (fractional!) warped location, recompute the residual, and repeat until the correction is smaller than \(\varepsilon\). Because the warped sample point is essentially never at an integer pixel, every one of those re-samples needs bilinear interpolation.

4. Bilinear interpolation

To evaluate \(I_2\) at a fractional coordinate \((x,y)\), let \(x_0=\lfloor x\rfloor,\ x_1=x_0+1,\ y_0=\lfloor y\rfloor,\ y_1=y_0+1\), and \(w_x = x-x_0,\ w_y = y-y_0\). Interpolate linearly along \(x\) at both rows, then linearly between those two results along \(y\):

$$R_0 = (1-w_x)I(x_0,y_0) + w_x I(x_1,y_0)$$

$$R_1 = (1-w_x)I(x_0,y_1) + w_x I(x_1,y_1)$$

$$I(x,y) \approx (1-w_y) R_0 + w_y R_1$$

Expanded, this is the familiar single bilinear form:

$$I(x,y)\approx I(x_0,y_0)(1{-}w_x)(1{-}w_y) + I(x_1,y_0)w_x(1{-}w_y) + I(x_0,y_1)(1{-}w_x)w_y + I(x_1,y_1)w_xw_y$$

Implemented from scratch in Homework/hw5_motion_sfm/bilinear.py (no cv2.remap) and verified against it in tests/test_hw5_bilinear.py - agreement to <0.01 intensity units at random fractional coordinates.
