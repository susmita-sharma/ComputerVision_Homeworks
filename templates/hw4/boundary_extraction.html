HW4 · Human Boundary Extraction

Two independent pipelines - one for regular RGB photos, one for thermal photos - find a human's exact boundary using only classical OpenCV operations (Otsu thresholding, morphology, watershed, contours). No machine learning or deep learning is used for the segmentation itself. Optionally upload a SAM2 mask (run separately - see note below) to compare against.

SAM2 does not run inside this app (it needs PyTorch + GPU-scale checkpoints). Generate a mask with the official SAM2 model in a notebook/Colab, save it as a black/white PNG (white = person), and upload it alongside the photo below to get IoU/Dice agreement metrics.

RGB photo

Pipeline: grayscale → Gaussian blur → Otsu threshold (foreground/background side picked automatically) → largest connected component → morphological close/open + hole fill → watershed refinement → external contour.

Theory: edge detection & segmentation in the frequency domain

Edge detection as high-pass filtering. The Fourier differentiation property says that differentiating in space corresponds to multiplying by frequency in the transform domain:

\( \mathcal{F}\Big\{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big\}(u,v) = (j2\pi u)\, F(u,v), \qquad \mathcal{F}\Big\{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Big\}(u,v) = (j2\pi v)\, F(u,v) \)

A gradient-based edge operator (Sobel/Prewitt) approximates ∂f/∂x and ∂f/∂y, so applying it is - in the frequency domain - equivalent to multiplying F(u,v) by j2πu or j2πv: a filter whose gain grows linearly with frequency and vanishes at u = 0 (a pure high-pass filter). That is precisely why edges (rapid intensity change = high spatial frequency) are amplified and flat regions (low frequency, including u=v=0, the DC/average brightness) are suppressed.

Laplacian. Applying the differentiation property twice gives the Laplacian's frequency response:

\( \mathcal{F}\{\nabla^2 f\}(u,v) = -4\pi^2 (u^2+v^2)\, F(u,v) \)

This is a radially symmetric high-pass filter (gain grows with distance from the origin in frequency space) - so the Laplacian edge detector is, in the frequency domain, just multiplying the spectrum by -4π²(u²+v²) and inverse-transforming. Equivalently, an edge map can be produced directly as

\( \text{edges}(x,y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\,H_{hp}(u,v)\, F(u,v)\,\} \)

for any high-pass Hhp (ideal, Gaussian, or Butterworth high-pass filters are the usual choices, trading ringing artifacts against transition sharpness).

Segmentation via frequency content. The complementary operation - low-pass filtering, Hlp(u,v) = 1 - Hhp(u,v) - suppresses the same high-frequency edge/texture energy and keeps smooth regions intact; this is exactly the blur used in HW3, and is often a pre-processing step before thresholding so that a segmentation boundary follows large-scale structure rather than noise. More generally, a region can be characterized by the local energy in different frequency bands (e.g. a bank of band-pass/Gabor filters): a pixel's feature vector is [\,|\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{H_i(u,v)F(u,v)\}(x,y)\,|]_i for a set of band-pass filters Hi, and pixels/regions are grouped by similarity of that vector - texture-based segmentation purely from Fourier-domain analysis, with no learned model involved.

In short: edges live at high spatial frequency, regions live at low spatial frequency, and both edge detection and region segmentation can be phrased as choosing the right H(u,v) to multiply the image's spectrum by before inverting.
